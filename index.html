Chapter 02 · Turning Points

SEVEN YEARS
THAT CHANGED
EVERYTHING

A handful of years reshaped the entire industry. New competitive titles entered the scene, crowdfunded prize pools shattered records, and a global pandemic pushed competition fully remote.

Scroll through the seven moments that defined esports history.

Annual prize money distributed

2000
Counter-Strike becomes one of the first major esports titles

Prize pools remain below $20,000, but organised events gain popularity in South Korea and Europe.

2006
Total annual prize money surpasses $1.5M for the first time

Esports organizations like the World Cyber Games bring international structure to competitive Counter-Strike and Warcraft III.

Smaller, regional scenes intertwine forming a global circuit, and the number of players nearly triples compared to the previous year.

2011
Riot Games launches League of Legends Esports

Riot Games funds prize pools and broadcasting events. By 2012, total prize money reaches $5.2M. League of Legends becomes the second-highest-earning title, generating over $90M across 2,743 tournaments.

2013
Dota 2's Crowdfunding Revolution

Valve introduces crowdfunded prize pools through Dota 2 esports. "The International 2013" raises $2.87M from fans alone.

Total annual prize money jumps to $17M in a single year, marking the largest increase so far.

2018
Fortnite's $100M Bet

Epic Games commits $100M to Fortnite prize pools, helping launch the battle royale era. Annual prize money reaches a record $123M.

The number of unique winners rises to 19,120, more than triple the previous year, as the genre attracts a large wave of new competitors.

2020
COVID-19 cancels in-person LAN events worldwide

Esports shifts to operating remotely. Prize money drops to $51M while remote tournament count remains high at 228 events.

Games like Fortnite and PUBG Mobile surge in popularity.

2023
A New Equilibrium

Prize money stabilises around $70M, spread across a record 258 tournaments and 13,600 winners. The industry becomes broader, more diverse, and less dependent on a handful of games.

Chapter 05 · The Digital Shift

ESPORTS SHIFTS
TO REMOTE PLAY

Before 2020, most prize money was awarded at live, in-person LAN (Local Area Network) events held in large venues. The pandemic shifted this balance quickly toward online play. Although in-person events have since returned, online competitions now represent a permanently larger share.

Notably, battle royale games like Fortnite were already mostly remote before the pandemic, while titles like Dota 2 preferred in-person events.

Conclusion · What the Data Shows

THE TAKEAWAY

01
Growth is publisher-driven

Every major prize money spike is tied to a deliberate publisher decision. Valve’s crowdfunding model in 2013, then Epic’s $100M Fortnite commitment in 2018. The industry does not grow organically; it grows when a single company makes a bet.

02
A few titles dominate earnings

Dota 2 alone accounts for over 27% of all prize money ever tracked, despite representing a small fraction of total tournaments. High-prestige, in-person championship formats concentrate money into fewer, larger events.

03
Online play is here to stay

The pandemic accelerated a shift that was already underway in battle royale titles. Online prize pools have held a larger share since 2020 even as in-person events returned, suggesting a permanently more distributed competitive landscape.

04
The industry is maturing

Post-2018, prize money stabilised rather than continuing to climb. The record 258 tournaments in 2023 suggest the industry is becoming broader and more distributed, less reliant on any single title’s generosity.
